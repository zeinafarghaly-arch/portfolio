The Problem

My FlyRank capstone produced a trained Logistic Regression model with a real result: 0.528 Precision@500 against a 0.490 baseline. But the model lived inside a private Colab session, trained on a warehouse I don't have standing access to outside the internship — nobody could actually use it. A research paper proves a model works. It doesn't hand anyone a tool. The question this project answers: how do I turn a one-off research result into something a real editor opens and uses?

What I Did: Reuse Honestly, Not Fake It

The exact serialized model (its scaler, its imputer statistics) never left that Colab session. I had two options: fake reloading a model I don't actually have, or be upfront about what I could honestly carry forward. I chose the second. The agent reuses the model's 6 real features and the signed, relative coefficient weights it learned from 9.8M real rows — turned into a transparent, deterministic scoring function instead of a black-box reload:

content_age_days

Weight −0.396 — the strongest signal, and counterintuitive: older content was less likely to be declining.

days_since_last_update

Weight +0.237 — the second strongest, and the intuitive one: untouched pages are more likely declining.

ctr, engagement_rate, search_volume, avg_position

Smaller, weaker-signal weights, still reused at the same relative scale as the original model.

What I Did: Score, Then Explain

The agent is deliberately two separate layers, not one black box:

1. scoring.py

A plain function. Normalizes the 6 features, applies the reused weights, outputs a 0–1 priority score. Fully deterministic — same input, same output, every time.

2. agent.py

Takes the tool's already-computed score and asks Claude to explain why a page landed there in plain language — Claude reasons over the number, it never invents its own.

3. app.py

Streamlit UI wrapping both — upload a CSV for a ranked shortlist, or score a single page manually.

Keeping scoring and reasoning as separate steps was the key design decision: it means the explanation can never quietly override or "improve" the score. What the editor sees explained is exactly what the model would have ranked.

What Came Of It

A publicly deployed tool, not just a notebook result: a live Streamlit app anyone can open, drop a CSV of pages into, and get back a ranked, explained shortlist — batch mode or single-page mode. It's the direct answer to the original problem: the FlyRank research paper now has a second, usable artifact standing next to it, built from the same real coefficients rather than a from-scratch demo.

Lessons Learned

  • A finished model isn't a finished project — the gap between "trained" and "usable" is its own piece of work.
  • Being upfront about what you can and can't honestly reuse (real weights, not a real reloaded pipeline) is more credible than pretending otherwise.
  • Separating the deterministic score from the AI's plain-language explanation keeps the tool trustworthy — Claude narrates the number, it doesn't get to change it.
  • Shipping something a non-technical editor can actually click and use is a different skill than getting a good Precision@500 — and just as necessary.
